faq-q: What does "have" mean in Basic English?
faq-a: Have is the verb of possession: to have a thing is to hold it, to own it, to keep it as yours. Its forms are have, has, having and had. It is word 13 of the 850 &mdash; and with it the book can finally say what belongs to whom: I have it; they have it; I had it.
faq-q: What is the difference between "have" and "keep"?
faq-a: Keep is have over time: you keep what you have, and you keep it safe. Have is the state itself &mdash; the thing is yours. Chapter 5 gave the hero the word keep; chapter 13 gives him the word have &mdash; first the state, then the guarding of it.
faq-q: Why do questions say "Do you have" and not "Have you"?
faq-a: In modern English, most questions with have take do-support: Do you have it? Do I have it? The verb do steps in front and carries the grammar, exactly as it did in chapter 12. Older English allowed Have you it? &mdash; you will still meet it in old books &mdash; but the living form is Do you have it?
faq-q: What does "had" give the story?
faq-a: Had is the past of have &mdash; what a person had before. The memory fragment uses it for the turn of the hidden novel: the hero had the thing, kept it, and gave it &mdash; so the loss he mourns began as his own act. I had it; I gave them it. The past tense makes ownership part of the plot.
faq-q: Why is part of the chapter in italics?
faq-a: Italics are memory. The hero recovers his past one fragment per chapter, told only with the words the book has given him so far &mdash; the fragments form a second, hidden story under the main one. With have comes had, and the fragment speaks of ownership: I had it; I let them have it; the having was the giving. What looked like theft begins to look like permission &mdash; and the mystery moves inside the hero.
