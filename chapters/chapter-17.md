faq-q: What does "may" mean in Basic English?
faq-a: May is the verb of leave and of chance. "May I see it?" asks for leave &mdash; it puts a question where an order might have been. "It may come" speaks of chance &mdash; the thing is possible, and nothing more. May is word 17 of the 850 &mdash; and with it the book gets its open door: the hero can ask, and the world can answer with a possibility instead of a fact.
faq-q: Why does "may" take no -s?
faq-a: May keeps one form for every person: I may, you may, he may, she may, it may, we may, they may. There is no "mays" and no "maying" &mdash; the word does not change, and the chapter says it flat, person after person, so the reader can see the flatness. Small words that never change are the hinges of Basic English; may is one of them.
faq-q: What is the difference between "may" and "will"?
faq-a: May opens a door and does not say what walks through it: "It may come" leaves the coming to chance. Will points: what will be, will be. In the story the two are night and morning &mdash; may is the hero asking "May I?" while nothing is decided yet; will (word 18, the next chapter) is the answer that fixes what will happen. The one asks; the other decides.
faq-q: How does "may" ask for leave?
faq-a: The question turns may around: "May I see it?" is the hero putting his seeing up for leave &mdash; he does not take; he asks. And the answer gives the leave back in the same small word: "You may." In the cave the hero has taken, made and sent; word 17 lets him stop and ask first &mdash; and the asking is itself a new kind of act.
faq-q: Why is part of the chapter in italics?
faq-a: Italics are memory. The hero recovers his past one fragment per chapter, told only with the words the book has given him so far &mdash; the fragments form a second, hidden story under the main one. With may the hidden story finds the paper trail of his sending: he asked for leave, they gave it, and their leave made the send. The permission was theirs; the going was his.
