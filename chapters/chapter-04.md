faq-q: What does "go" mean in Basic English?
faq-a: To go means to move away, to travel from one place to another. Together with come (word 1) it forms the first pair of opposites in the book: come moves toward the speaker, go moves away.
faq-q: Why does the story repeat "I go — it comes"?
faq-a: Because the two verbs chase each other. Every time the hero moves away, the unseen presence follows. The chapter turns a grammar pair into a chase — and the chase is the cliffhanger: you cannot get far from something that always comes.
faq-q: Does "go" change its form?
faq-a: Yes, and irregularly: go, goes, going — but the past is went, not goed, and there is gone as well. Went is one of the most famous irregular forms in English, and the book gives it to you in context, not as a table.
faq-q: Why is there no "to" in "I go"?
faq-a: The word to is word 35 of the 850, and chapter 4 may only use words 1 to 4. English happily says I go with no destination named — and that gap becomes part of the story: the hero goes, but where is not said. When to finally arrives at word 35, the same sentence will grow into I go to the sea — and you will feel the new word click into a slot that has been waiting for it.
faq-q: What is "the going"?
faq-a: The verb turned into a thing — a gerund. English does this freely: the going goes, the coming comes. Ogden's list loves such moves, because one verb plus this trick can cover dozens of nouns like trip, walk and departure.
